JPEG XL vs WebP

JPEG XL is the better format. WebP is the more useful one. That contradiction is the entire comparison, and which side of it matters depends on one question: does the image need to open on someone else's computer?

The verdict, up front

Publishing images on a website? Use WebP. Every browser in use today renders it, and Chrome still has JPEG XL turned off by default. A better format that your visitors cannot see is not a better format.

Storing, archiving or editing your own photos? Use JPEG XL. It compresses harder at the quality levels you'd actually keep photos at, it holds HDR and high bit depths that WebP simply discards, and it can re-pack your existing JPEG library losslessly — which WebP cannot do at all.

A note on the numbers you'll see quoted

Search for this comparison and you will find confident claims that JPEG XL is "35% smaller than WebP", or 20%, or 50%. Treat all of them carefully, including any we could have put here.

Image compression results depend on the photograph, the quality target, the encoder version and the effort setting — and on how quality was matched between two formats, which is the part almost nobody states. A comparison at "quality 80" in each encoder is meaningless, because the two scales are unrelated. Done properly you need a perceptual metric and a corpus of images, and the honest output is a curve, not a headline percentage.

What is safe to say: at the quality levels people actually keep photographs at, JPEG XL is usually meaningfully smaller than WebP; in lossless mode the gap widens; and at aggressively low quality the two converge. If the exact number matters to you, the only answer worth having is the one from your images — so run a few of them through the converter and compare the file sizes yourself. It's free and nothing gets uploaded.

The one thing WebP cannot do at all

Everything above is a matter of degree. This one isn't.

JPEG XL can take an existing JPEG and re-pack it losslessly — roughly 20% smaller, with the original JPEG reconstructible byte for byte. Not "visually identical". The same file back.

For anyone with a photo library of tens of thousands of JPEGs, this is the single most useful thing either format does: about a fifth of the storage back, no quality decision to agonise over, and no lock-in, since you can always unpack to the original. Converting those JPEGs to WebP would mean re-compressing them — throwing away quality to save space, permanently. Try it on one of your own JPEGs and watch the size drop without touching a quality slider.

Which one should you pick?

🌍

Website images → WebP

Universal support, good compression, no fallback machinery to maintain. Revisit this when Chrome enables JPEG XL by default.

📸

Photo archive → JPEG XL

Lossless JPEG re-packing, HDR preserved, better compression where it counts. You control the software that opens them.

🎨

Graphics & screenshots → JPEG XL

WebP's lossy mode is locked to 4:2:0 chroma, which smears coloured text and sharp edges. JPEG XL can keep full colour resolution.

🖼️

Huge panoramas → JPEG XL

WebP stops at 16,383 pixels per side. Stitched panoramas and high-resolution scans hit that wall regularly.

📧

Sending to someone → neither

Send a JPEG or PNG. Plenty of people still can't open a WebP either, let alone a .jxl.

🎞️

Animation → either, carefully

Both support it, both are better than GIF, and neither is well supported by editing tools. For the web, WebP is the pragmatic pick.

FAQ

Will JPEG XL replace WebP?
Not soon, and possibly never outright. WebP is entrenched, works everywhere and is good enough for most web images. JPEG XL's realistic path is to win storage, archival and professional workflows first, and web delivery only if and when browser support becomes universal.
Is it worth converting my WebP files to JPEG XL?
Usually not. A lossy WebP has already thrown away detail; converting it to lossy JPEG XL throws away a little more for a modest size gain. If you still have the original photo, convert that instead. Lossless WebP files are a reasonable exception since no quality is lost either way.
Which is faster to encode?
JPEG XL, comfortably — and much faster than AVIF, which is its other main rival. WebP is quick too. Encoding speed only really bites when you are processing thousands of images at once.
What about AVIF?
AVIF is the third option and a serious one: real browser support today, and very strong compression at low quality. It is slower to encode, weaker at high quality and lossless, and has no equivalent of JPEG XL's lossless JPEG re-packing.
