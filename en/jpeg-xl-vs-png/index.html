JPEG XL vs PNG

Both are lossless, so you get back exactly the same pixels. We checked that on all 20 test images, then measured what it costs. On photos and illustrations JPEG XL wins clearly. On screenshots, a well-optimised PNG beat it unless we let JPEG XL work much harder.

by Robert Castillo · Published October 15, 2026

📷 Photos: −33% vs best PNG🫥 Illustrations: −41%🖥️ Screenshots: PNG wins at default✅ 0 pixels changed

The short answer

For photos and for our illustrations, JPEG XL is clearly smaller. At its default settings it shrank our photos by a third and our illustrations by 41% (43% for the three with real transparency) compared with PNGs already optimised with oxipng's maximum level (-o max, without its slower Zopfli mode), and every pixel came back identical.

For screenshots it is not that simple. At the default effort JPEG XL was bigger than a well-optimised PNG on three of our five screenshots. It only pulled clearly ahead at effort 10, its slowest setting, where in total it cut them by 53% (from 12% to 75% depending on the screenshot). And PNG decodes roughly ten times faster and opens absolutely everywhere, which still makes it the sensible choice for anything you are about to share.

What we tested

Twenty images of four kinds. Eight photos from phones and cameras, all public domain (CC0) from Wikimedia Commons, reduced to 2048 pixels on the long side. Five screenshots of our own website at normal, 2× (laptop) and 3× (phone) pixel density, plus terminal output and a page of code rendered in the browser. Three charts we generated with matplotlib. And four illustrations from Wikimedia Commons, CC0, rendered from SVG; three have a transparent background and soft edges, the fourth, a map, is almost entirely opaque.

Each one was stored as the PNG the program that made it wrote (ImageMagick, Chrome, matplotlib or cairosvg), the "unoptimised" PNG most people actually have; through oxipng 10.2.1 at its default level and at its maximum; and as lossless JPEG XL (cjxl -d 0) at efforts 1, 2, 3, 7 (the default) and 9, plus effort 10 for everything except the photos, where it takes minutes per image.

For photos, effort 1 is the surprise: 13% smaller than the best PNG while using about 160 times less processor time than oxipng -o max. Effort 3 gets most of the way to the default result for a fifth of the time. Above effort 7 there is almost nothing left to gain on photos: effort 9 saved another 0.6% for six times the work, and on one photo we also tried effort 10, which saved 3% more than effort 9 for roughly four times as long again (about two minutes of processor time for 3.1 megapixels).

For screenshots, charts and flat-colour graphics it is the opposite: the low efforts are worse than PNG and the high ones are far better. If you archive screenshots as JPEG XL, use effort 9 or 10 and be patient. Note also that oxipng's default level is almost as good as its maximum, at a tenth of the time.

Is it really lossless? And transparency?

Yes, on everything we tried. We decoded every JPEG XL file (112 of them, across all effort levels) and compared it with the original PNG pixel by pixel, red, green, blue and alpha, with ImageMagick. The number of pixels that differed was zero in every single case. The same check on the oxipng files also gave zero.

That includes the three illustrations with real transparency: the soft, semi-transparent edges round the astronaut, the elephant and the robot came back exactly, and as a group they were where JPEG XL gained most (43% at the default effort). Our three charts carry an alpha channel that is fully opaque, which is what matplotlib writes; both formats kept it without trouble, so you don't need to strip it before converting.

All of this is cjxl on the command line. Our PNG to JPEG XL converter works differently: it is lossy unless you tick “Lossless”, it uses a fast effort setting, and it reads the image through the browser's canvas, which works in 8 bits and can round semi-transparent pixels. For an exact copy of a 16-bit or transparent image, use cjxl -d 0.

Decoding: PNG is about ten times faster

This is the cost nobody puts on the box. On one core, djxl needed 0.33 seconds per megapixel to decode our lossless photos and around 0.19 for the other images. Opening the same pictures as PNG with Pillow, which uses the standard zlib library, took between 0.014 and 0.035 seconds per megapixel.

For a single image on screen that is the difference between a fraction of a second and an instant. For a folder of thousands of files being thumbnailed, or a web page full of large lossless graphics, it is noticeable. Decoders can use several cores, and browsers and viewers do, which narrows the wait, but the work is still about ten times greater.

If your PNG is a photo, ask whether it needs to be lossless

Photos often end up as PNG for no better reason than a screenshot tool or an editor's default. Lossless storage of a photo is expensive in any format: our eight photos took 2.7 MB each on average as lossless JPEG XL.

In our JPEG XL vs JPEG test, the same photos encoded with lossy JPEG XL at visually lossless quality (an SSIMULACRA2 score of 90, where you cannot spot a difference even flicking between the two) came out a median 3.3 times smaller than lossless, and at the still excellent score of 85 about 5.5 times smaller. If the PNG is a master you will edit again, keep it lossless. If it is a photo you just want to keep, lossy JPEG XL at -d 0.5 to -d 1 is the bigger saving by far.

How we measured

Machine and timing: a shared Ubuntu 24.04 server with 6 virtual AMD EPYC cores. Every command ran on one thread (cjxl --num_threads=0, oxipng -t 1), one after another, capped at 2.5 GB of memory and at the lowest priority, so the server's websites came first. Times are processor time (user plus system), which other load on the machine barely affects; read them as ratios rather than as what your own computer will do.

Tools: libjxl 0.12.0 (cjxl, djxl), oxipng 10.2.1, ImageMagick 6.9.12 for resizing and for the pixel comparison (compare -metric AE on all four channels), Pillow 12.3 for PNG decoding times, Chrome 146 for the screenshots, matplotlib 3.10.8 for the charts and cairosvg 2.9.0 for the illustrations.

Commands: oxipng -t 1 -o 2 and oxipng -t 1 -o max; cjxl in.png out.jxl -d 0 -e E --num_threads=0 with E = 1, 2, 3, 7, 9 and 10; djxl out.jxl check.png for the pixel check and djxl out.jxl out.pam --num_threads=0 for decoding time.

Limits: this is a small sample, not a study. We planned 16 photos and ran 8 (four from phones, four from cameras) because the test had to be slowed down and run one command at a time on a server that also hosts websites; effort 10 was not run on photos. We did not test tiny images such as icons, where file overhead matters more, nor PNG's slowest optimiser, zopfli. The photos come from JPEGs and were reduced, so they are cleaner than a raw development; real lossless photo files may compress differently. Scripts, results per image as CSV, and the source and licence of every image are kept with the measurements.

Common questions

Is JPEG XL smaller than PNG?
For photos and illustrations, yes, clearly: a third smaller for our photos and 41% for our illustrations at the default setting, against PNGs already optimised with oxipng. For screenshots, only at the slowest effort; at the default it was larger than a well-optimised PNG on three of five.
Is lossless JPEG XL really lossless?
Yes. We decoded all 112 files from our test and compared them with the originals pixel by pixel, including the alpha channel. Not a single pixel differed.
Does JPEG XL keep transparency?
Yes, exactly. Our three illustrations with soft transparent edges came back identical, and as a group they were where JPEG XL saved the most.
Which effort should I use for lossless?
For photos, the default (7) or even 3 if you are in a hurry; above 7 the gain is tiny. For screenshots and flat graphics, 9 or 10, because the lower settings can end up bigger than PNG.
Should I convert all my PNGs to JPEG XL?
Only the ones you keep for yourself. Anything you share or upload is safer as PNG, which opens everywhere and decodes about ten times faster.
