HEIC vs JPG

One of these saves half your storage. The other one actually opens. That's the whole argument, and which one wins depends entirely on what you're about to do with the photo.

by Robert Castillo · Published August 29, 2026

The short answer

Keep HEIC on your phone. Send JPG to other people. That single rule covers almost every situation you'll run into, and it's what Apple itself does when you share a photo through most apps.

The reasoning is simple. Storage on your phone is finite and HEIC halves what each photo costs you. But the moment a photo leaves your device it lands somewhere you don't control — a Windows PC, a web form, someone's ancient Android — and there JPG is the only format guaranteed to work.

When HEIC is the right answer

📱

Photos staying on your phone

Thousands of pictures you'll never send anywhere. Halving their size is free storage.

🌇

Skies, sunsets, gradients

10-bit colour means noticeably less banding in smooth transitions than 8-bit JPEG.

🗄️

Archiving originals

If you're keeping the master copy, keep the one with more information in it.

When JPG is the right answer

📤

Sending to anyone

Email, WhatsApp to a non-iPhone, a website form, a print shop. JPG never fails.

🪟

Anything touching Windows

Especially Windows 10, where HEIC often demands a paid codec before it will display.

🖨️

Printing and paperwork

Photo labs, official forms and older software frequently accept nothing else.

What you lose converting HEIC to JPG

Converting downgrades the file, and it's worth being honest about that rather than pretending it's free. You go from a modern compressor to one designed in 1992, so the resulting JPG is bigger and slightly worse at the same time. You also drop from 10-bit colour to 8-bit, and any extra frames — the movement in a Live Photo, the alternative shots in a burst — are discarded, because a JPG holds exactly one image.

In practice, for a photo you're about to send to someone, none of this is visible. The loss matters when you're converting your whole library and deleting the originals. Don't do that: convert copies, keep the HEIC files.

And where does JPEG XL fit?

JPEG XL is the format that would settle this argument if support caught up. It matches HEIC's compression without the patent problems that made Windows charge for a codec, it handles both lossy and lossless in one format, and it can re-pack an existing JPEG about 20% smaller without touching a single pixel.

The catch in 2026 is the same one HEIC has, only worse: Safari displays it, most other software still doesn't. It's an excellent choice for archiving, and a bad one for sending a photo to your mother.

What happens to the date, location and other details

Both formats carry EXIF metadata — the date the photo was taken, the camera settings, and the GPS location if you had it enabled — and a straightforward conversion between them keeps that information intact. Your photo library will not suddenly lose track of when everything happened.

Two things do need watching. Some conversion tools strip metadata as a privacy measure, which is helpful if you are about to post the photo publicly and unhelpful if you are archiving. And HEIC can store depth maps and the edits you applied in the Photos app as separate, reversible layers; a JPG cannot, so those get flattened into the final image and you lose the ability to undo them later.

If the photos matter, the safe pattern is the same as always: convert a copy for sharing and leave the original where it is.

Common questions

Does converting keep the photo’s date and location?
Yes, a normal conversion preserves the EXIF data. What it cannot preserve are HEIC-only extras like depth maps and reversible edits, which get flattened into the image.
Is HEIC better quality than JPG?
At the same file size, yes — noticeably. HEIC also carries 10-bit colour against JPEG's 8-bit. But 'better' stops mattering if the file won't open where you need it.
Should I convert my whole photo library to JPG?
No. Convert the ones you're sending and keep the originals. Converting everything doubles your storage and permanently loses colour depth for no benefit.
Does converting HEIC to JPG make the file bigger?
Usually yes, often close to double. JPEG needs more space for the same picture because its compression is thirty years older.
Which should I choose in my iPhone settings?
«High Efficiency» (HEIC) if storage matters and you mostly stay in Apple's world. «Most Compatible» (JPEG) if you constantly send photos to Windows or Android users.
Do I lose Live Photos converting to JPG?
Yes. A JPG holds one frame. The original HEIC keeps the movement, so don't delete it.
Can I convert JPG back to HEIC?
You can, but it won't restore anything: the colour depth and detail thrown away by the JPEG step are gone. Converting back only re-compresses what's left.
